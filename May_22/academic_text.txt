Here again, some sort of adjustment costs lie behind the theory. If a firm can freely change its capital stock, then it will continue to increase or decrease its capital stock until q is equal to unity. Also, the role of the production function is never clear in Tobin's [15] exposition. One may wonder if the "q" theory can be derived from the firm's optimization. It is increasingly recognized that the modified neoclassical investment theory with installment costs and the "q" theory are equivalent. Lucas and Prescott [14] were the first to recognize this, although they never indicated the connection to the "q" theory. Later Abel [1] showed that the optimal rate of investment is the rate for which q - 1 is equal to the marginal cost of installment. However, his discussion is focused primarily on the Cobb-Douglas technology. Yoshikawa [18] arrived at the same conclusion as Abel did, but his model is characterized by static expectations. Section 2 of the present paper integrates the two theories of investment in a very general model of the firm's present value maximization and derives the optimal rate of investment as a function of q. It turns out that the form of investment function is independent of both the production function and the demand curve for the firm's output. All this comes from a simple application of Pontryagin's maximum principle. The "q" theory (or, equivalently, the modified neoclassical theory) is not operational as long as q is not observable. Remember that q, which we call marginal q, is the ratio of the market value of an additional unit of capital to its replacement cost. What we can observe is average q, namely the ratio of the market value of existing capital to its replacement cost. Empirical work based on the "q" theory has utilized average q as a proxy for marginal q (see, e.g., von Furstenberg [8]). Section 3 of the present paper derives an exact relationship between marginal q and average q. If the firm is a price-taker with constant returns to scale in both production and installation, then marginal q is equal to average q. For example, Brainard and Tobin (1968) write, “There are many kinds of physical capital and many markets where existing stocks are valued… .” This observation implies each different type of asset should have a different Tobin’s q. Implementing this observation is difficult, however, because the stock market values a firm’s aggregate assets, not any one particular class of assets. Nonetheless, two different Tobin’s q’s have emerged in the literature. The first, used primarily in corporate finance, is the ratio of the market value of the firm to the replacement cost of all the firm’s assets. The second, used primarily in the macroeconomics literature to explain investment in tangible fixed capital, is the ratio of the market value of the firm’s stock of such capital to that stock’s replacement value. Because the issues in estimating Tobin’s q differ for the macro and finance proxies, we deal with the two sets of issues separately. Most researchers estimate macro Tobin’s q by evaluating a variant of the expression: (1) in which D is the market value of debt for firm i, S is the market value of its equity, N is the i i i replacement value of its inventories, and K is the replacement value of its capital stock. i The numerator only approximates the market value of the capital stock. The market values of debt and equity equal the market value of the firm. If inventories and capital are the firm’s only assets, the market value of the capital stock is correctly obtained by subtracting inventories from the value of the firm. However, if the firm holds nonphysical assets such as human capital, intangibles, and goodwill, this measure of the market value of the capital stock will be biased upward. Data limitations usually prevent estimating the value of nonphysical assets with any useful precision. This omission is one reason that estimated and true macro Tobin’s q can differ. The other reason is that the replacement value of assets and the market value of debt are not directly observable and must be estimated. Whereas macro q describes the incentive to invest in tangible fixed capital, finance q characterizes the incentive to invest in all types of assets. This requirement stems from the nature of research in corporate finance. 4. Our results show that while accounting for mis-measurement - by using Fitted Q - is helpful, addressing model mis-specification - by using marginal q - is far more important. Beyondthisnewmethodology, ourpaperalsomakesanumberofimportant empirical contributions. First, using a novel statistical test - qQ test - we document that marginal q is statistically different from (average) Tobin’s Q or its measurement error-free counterpart, Fitted Q. On average, marginal q is substantially lower (0.85 vs 1.75 vs 3.57), and less volatile (0.69 vs 1.98 vs 8.22) than both Fitted Q and (average) Tobin’s Q, respectively. We also show that marginal q only accounts for a small amount of the variation in (average) Tobin’s Q, and to less extent in Fittted Q. Hence, using (average) Tobin’s Q, or its measurement error-free counterpart, instead of marginal q may lead to incorrect inference. Second, our empirical estimates of marginal q rather than Tobin’s Q, or its measurement error-free counterpart, provide much tighter (and much more plausible) quasi-model free estimates of capital adjustment costs. Conven- tional measures of Tobin’s Q imply estimates of (maximum) adjustment costs that are 3.2 times larger than the ones we obtain when estimating marginal q directly. Third, we find that investment is substantially more responsive to changes in marginal q. We start with these statistics because many researchers have assessed the quality of a proxy for q by whether its mean or median is close to unity. The reasoning comes directly from Tobin (1969), who stated that a firm should invest only if Tobin’s q is greater than one. This statement implies that the equilibrium value for Tobin’s q is, in fact, one. Given this intuition, it appears that the finance proxies in the top panel of the table are “better” than the macro proxies in the bottom panel, because their medians, and especially their means, are closer to one. In fact, however, the median and mean of average q should be greater than one for several reasons. The first is the presence of adjustment and installation costs in the investment process. Because of these costs, even though the firm starts to invest when its average q rises, adjustment costs prevent instantaneous capital accumulation, and q moves back down to unity slowly. The second is sample selection within an industry. Specifically, unprofitable firms with very low average q’s exit, and we only observe the profitable firms with higher average q’s . These two reasons pertain to true average q. A third reason is that the proxy may be an upward biased estimate of Tobin’s q, and hence of average q; in terms of Equation (3), this would be the case in which γ > 0. For example, we suspect strongly that the higher means and medians for the macro proxies result from failing to subtract intangible assets from the numerator of Equation (1). All of these reasons imply that simply looking at the median or mean of a proxy in order to assess its measurement quality can be misleading. One final feature of Table I is important.
